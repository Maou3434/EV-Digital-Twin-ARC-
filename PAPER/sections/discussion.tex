\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Rollout Performance and Exposure Bias}
The contrast between single-step accuracy and multi-step rollout trajectory drift illustrates the challenge of dynamic system rollouts. At high sampling frequencies ($dt = 0.01$~s), temperature differences between adjacent steps are small. An unconstrained feedforward network can exploit an identity shortcut ($\Delta T \approx 0$), achieving low single-step loss ($< 5 \times 10^{-7}$$^\circ$C) without capturing underlying dynamic thermal relationships.

During multi-step rollout execution, errors compound across thousands of recursive steps. Injecting Gaussian noise ($\sigma = 0.05$$^\circ$C) into the temperature input feature during training perturbs the model's state space, forcing it to learn error-correcting trajectories and reducing maximum rollout drift from 0.2474$^\circ$C to 0.0851$^\circ$C on unseen test cycles.

\subsection{Effective Parameter Estimation and Core-Surface Dynamics}
In our experiments, the trainable convective heat transfer parameter ($hA$) converged to an effective value of $36.49$~W/K. It is essential to recognize that because the target dataset is generated from a dual-node Simulink model (tracking distinct core temperature $T_c$ and surface temperature $T_s$), fitting a single-node thermal ODE ($m C_p \frac{dT}{dt} = P_{\text{loss}} - hA (T - T_{\text{amb}})$) forces $hA$ to act as an \textbf{effective parameter}.

During unconstrained optimization, core-to-surface thermal phase lags—wherein surface temperature $T_s$ continues rising due to residual core heat flow after current load drops ($P_{\text{loss}} = 0$)—can push single-node linear regression fits toward negative convection coefficients ($hA < 0$). A negative coefficient represents a structural discrepancy between a single-node model formulation and dual-node thermal dynamics. Enforcing $hA = \text{softplus}(hA_{\text{raw}}) > 0$ prevents physically impossible negative cooling values while yielding an effective heat transfer parameter consistent with forced cooling.

\subsection{Component-wise Architecture Analysis}
The structural role of each component within the digital twin framework is summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Feedforward Network:} Offers high-speed evaluation, but lacks trajectory-correcting feedback mechanisms, making it vulnerable to recursive rollout drift under exposure bias.
    \item \textbf{Input Noise Injection:} Exposes the network to state perturbations during training ($\sigma = 0.05$$^\circ$C), training it to correct trajectory deviations and stabilizing multi-step rollouts.
    \item \textbf{Physics Loss Regularization ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$):} Softly regularizes model gradients against the thermal energy balance ODE, ensuring that predicted temperature changes stay bounded by physical loss rates.
    \item \textbf{Softplus Parameter Constraint:} Restricts the trainable convective parameter $hA = \text{softplus}(hA_{\text{raw}}) > 0$, avoiding gradient clipping at zero and preventing non-physical negative cooling estimates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis of Model Failure Modes and Transient Limitations}
An inspection of rollout trajectory error spikes highlights specific operating regimes where prediction drift increases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rapid Current Step Transients:} Under sharp acceleration spikes (e.g., power bursts during US06 or WLTP2 high-speed phases), rapid increases in heat generation ($P_{\text{loss}}$) cause transient heat flux delays. The observed trajectory deviations in these peak regions are consistent with core-to-surface thermal lags that cannot be fully captured by a single-node ODE formulation.
    \item \textbf{Ambient Temperature Extremes:} When evaluating extreme ambient cooling conditions ($0^\circ$C vs. $50^\circ$C), fixed effective physical parameters ($hA$) introduce minor localized deviations during rapid cooling transitions, suggesting that temperature-dependent convective models may be beneficial under severe environmental shifts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
To maintain scientific transparency, explicit limitations of this study are acknowledged:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Simulator Data Source:} Both training and evaluation datasets were generated from the same underlying Simulink electro-thermal model (although tested on unseen WLTP2 drive cycles). Cross-simulator and physical battery hardware validation in a climate chamber remain essential future work.
    \item \textbf{Single-Node Model Simplification:} The physics loss incorporates a single-node lumped thermal ODE. While computationally efficient for real-time edge use, it omits internal spatial temperature gradients and core-surface thermal lag dynamics.
    \item \textbf{Single Unseen Drive Cycle Test:} Generalization was evaluated across 87 test files covering the WLTP2 cycle. Broader testing across arbitrary synthetic load profiles remains to be evaluated.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Battery Aging Dynamics:} Internal resistance and thermal capacity are treated as constant, omitting state-of-health (SOH) degradation over battery service life.
    \item \textbf{No Real-Time Online Adaptation:} Physical parameters are estimated offline during training and remain static during rollout inference, rather than adapting dynamically via online state estimation (e.g., Extended Kalman Filtering).
    \item \textbf{Single-Seed Benchmarking:} Reported metrics reflect single training runs per architecture. Multi-seed statistical confidence intervals represent an important direction for future benchmarking.
    \item \textbf{Baseline Architecture Scope:} Comparisons were conducted against a feedforward MLP baseline to isolate physics regularization effects. Comparative evaluation against recurrent (LSTM/GRU), Transformer, and Neural ODE architectures is reserved for future work.
\end{enumerate}
