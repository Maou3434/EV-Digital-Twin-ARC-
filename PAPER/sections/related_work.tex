\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Traditional Battery Thermal Modeling}
Traditional battery thermal models can be broadly divided into numerical models and lumped-parameter models. Numerical methods, such as Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) and Finite Element Methods (FEM), solve three-dimensional heat conduction and fluid convection equations over discretized cell geometries \cite{zhang2017surrogate, ouyang2022surrogate}. Although these models provide detailed spatial temperature distributions, their computational complexity prevents real-time execution in vehicle microcontrollers. 

To bridge this gap, researchers deploy lumped-parameter models. The simplest is the single-node lumped capacitance model, which assumes uniform cell temperature and models the heat transfer via a single thermal capacitance and a convection resistance. For cylindrical or large-format pouch cells where a significant temperature gradient exists between the internal core and outer surface, dual-node models (representing the cell core and casing separately) are used \cite{saha2014lumped}. Equivalent circuit models (ECMs) are also widely used, mapping thermal behaviors to electrical resistances and capacitances \cite{kaliaperumal2024thermal, xie2021enhanced, li2021novel}. While these models run in real time, they require parameter identification over narrow operating envelopes, and their accuracy degrades under dynamic transient cycles.

\subsection{Data-Driven and Digital Twin Models}
With the abundance of battery sensor data (voltage, current, temperature), machine learning models have been applied to thermal state prediction. Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Transformers are utilized to predict future cell temperatures or degradation trajectories \cite{saravanan2022machine}. These models form the core intelligence of battery digital twins, enabling predictive maintenance and state estimation \cite{lyu2024digitaltwin, patil2025review, semeraro2024battery, li2021review}. 

However, purely data-driven models are black boxes. They do not enforce physical constraints, meaning they can output predictions that violate conservation laws when exposed to out-of-distribution (OOD) data, such as extreme temperatures or novel drive cycles \cite{roberts2026relevance}.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)}
Physics-Informed Neural Networks, popularized by Raissi et al. \cite{raissi2019physicsinformed}, incorporate physical laws (expressed as ordinary or partial differential equations) into the neural network training loop. By penalizing violations of these equations, PINNs regularize the search space, reducing data requirements and improving generalization.

In battery systems, PINNs have been applied to state-of-charge (SOC) and state-of-health (SOH) estimation by embedding electrochemical equations like Fick's law of diffusion or the single particle model \cite{li2021physics, li2023hybrid, pollo2024coupling, ke2026physics}. For thermal management, PINNs have been utilized to estimate spatial temperature distributions or predict thermal anomalies \cite{chen2022physics, wang2024physicsinformed, ji2025physics, saha2026physics, paoletti2025multip, ji2025realtime}. 

\subsection{Exposure Bias and Drift in Dynamic Rollout}
In dynamic time-series modeling, models are often trained using teacher forcing, which optimizes single-step prediction errors \cite{ranzato2015sequence}. During inference, however, the model runs autoregressively, feeding its own previous outputs as inputs. This mismatch, known as exposure bias, leads to error accumulation and trajectory drift \cite{ranzato2015sequence}.

While exposure bias is heavily researched in natural language processing and video generation \cite{bengio2015scheduled}, it remains an under-addressed challenge in physical system rollouts. A battery thermal digital twin must predict trajectories over thousands of seconds ($dt = 0.01$s corresponds to $150,000$ steps for a 1500s cycle). Under such conditions, even sub-millikelvin single-step errors compound rapidly. In this paper, we study this exposure bias under physical constraints and demonstrate that injecting Gaussian noise into the input temperature during training effectively mitigates this cumulative drift.
